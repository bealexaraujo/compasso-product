# User Research
## Compasso — Discovery & Validation

---

## Research Context

This document synthesizes the user research that informed Compasso's product direction. It draws from two sources: the founder's direct personal experience as a practicing psychologist navigating fiscal obligations as a Pessoa Física, and informal peer conversations with fellow psychologists from the same graduating class.

This is not a formal research study. It is an honest account of what was learned (from lived experience and from peer conversations) and how that learning shaped the product.

---

## Methodology

**Source 1 — Founder's personal experience**
The product concept originated from a real fiscal crisis experienced by the founder after graduating and starting clinical practice. The experience is documented here as a primary research input, not a marketing narrative.

**Source 2 — Peer conversations**
Informal group conversations with psychologist colleagues from the same graduating class (approximately 60 people, ranging from 22 to 60+ years old at graduation). The conversations were not scripted or structured as formal interviews. They emerged organically when the founder raised the topic of fiscal obligations, and the group's reaction itself became one of the most revealing data points in the research.

**Scope**
All participants were psychologists operating as Pessoa Física (PF), without CNPJ, in autonomous practice in Brazil. Income levels varied, but the fiscal situation was structurally similar across participants.

---

## Participant Overview

| Profile | Description |
|---|---|
| Founder | Practicing psychologist, graduated and immediately opened a private practice. Built a patient base quickly. Had no fiscal guidance and operated under wrong assumptions for over a year before discovering the problem. |
| Colleagues with established patient load | Had the same profile as the founder. Several were in the middle of paying off debt from tax regularization negotiations at the time of the conversation. |
| Colleagues with fewer patients | Earlier in practice. Less exposed to the problem so far, but immediately anxious upon learning what obligations they were missing. |

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Everyone was equally lost, but nobody knew that

The single most striking moment in the research happened not through any individual conversation, but through a group dynamic. When the founder raised the topic of fiscal obligations in a peer group, a conversation that had been quiet suddenly became impossible to stop. Everyone had something to say. Everyone had the same story.

> *"I had no idea you guys were going through it too."*

> *"I was ashamed of talking about it."*

The problem was universal, but it had been completely invisible because nobody talked about it. The shame of not knowing prevented people from discovering they weren't alone. This dynamic allowed the problem to compound silently across an entire professional cohort.

**Product implication:** The audience is not a small segment of disorganized professionals. It is the default state of autonomous psychologists in Brazil. The problem is structural, not personal.

---

### 2. The "CRP + RPA" myth was almost universal

Nearly all participants believed the same thing: that annual CRP dues and issuing a simple RPA (Recibo de Pagamento Autônomo) for each patient was sufficient to be fiscally compliant.

This belief was wrong. Most were not filing Carnê-Leão. Most were not registered on Receita Saúde. Most were not contributing to INSS. Some were not declaring income to IRPF at all, or were declaring it incorrectly.

Nobody arrived at this misunderstanding through carelessness. They simply didn't know what they didn't know, and the information available to them did not correct this.

> *"I thought CRP and the RPA were enough."*

**Product implication:** The onboarding cannot assume any prior fiscal knowledge. It must identify what the user is currently doing and surface the gap between that and what is actually required, without judgment.

---

### 3. The information environment actively misled professionals

When participants tried to find information on their own (through internet searches, accounting firms, or company websites) they consistently received misleading guidance.

Content found online was predominantly written by accounting companies whose business model depends on PF professionals opening CNPJ. The content framed CNPJ as the only safe option and avoided giving clear, actionable guidance on PF-specific obligations.

Direct contact with accounting firms produced the same result. Firms would point toward risks, suggest CNPJ as the solution, and decline to explain what the PF obligations actually were.

> *"I contacted all these big firms (Contabilizei, Contajá, Agilize and others), and all of them gave me half information, always telling me I should open a CNPJ or I could have problems. They were not teaching me what was necessary. They were selling their product."*

One participant did hire a firm. The firm opened a CNPJ for her, which came with ongoing monthly accounting fees she could not easily afford on her clinic income. She was paying for professional accounting services on an income that did not require them, a direct result of having no access to honest, independent guidance.

**Product implication:** Trust is the primary product problem, not awareness. Compasso must communicate that it exists to serve PF professionals who are not required to open CNPJ, and that its job is to make their actual obligations clear, not to sell them anything beyond that.

---

### 4. The reckoning is always expensive, and always late

The founder's fiscal situation went undetected for over a year. When it was finally discovered (through an aunt who happened to be an accountant) multiple obligations had accumulated across multiple periods. The amount due could not be paid at once and required negotiated payment terms stretched over many months.

This pattern was common among colleagues. Several were still paying their own regularization negotiations at the time of the conversations. The discovery trigger was never proactive. It was always external: a family member who happened to be an accountant, a colleague who mentioned they'd been caught, a tax notice.

> *"It was a lot of money that I didn't have. She helped me negotiate the value over many months. It got me truly traumatized."*

There is a structural reason for this. When the Brazilian tax authority identifies an issue (a missing payment, an incorrect declaration, an unfiled obligation), the notification does not arrive by email or phone call. It is posted inside government systems like e-CAC (the Receita Federal's online portal). A professional who has never accessed e-CAC, and doesn't know it exists, will never see those notices. The system assumes the taxpayer is already monitoring it. For autonomous psychologists with no fiscal guidance, that assumption fails completely.

**Product implication:** Compasso's core value is not solving the fiscal problem after it accumulates. It is preventing the accumulation in the first place. The product needs to be positioned accordingly, as something that makes the reckoning unnecessary, not just less painful.

---

### 5. Psychologists did not expect to manage administrative complexity

Choosing psychology as a profession does not come with an expectation of becoming a secretary, marketing professional, content creator, and accountant simultaneously. The professional training focused entirely on clinical practice.

This was consistent across all age groups in the graduating class, from 22-year-olds to 60-year-olds. The fiscal gap was not a generational issue or a digital literacy issue. It was a structural gap in professional formation that affected everyone equally.

> *"When we choose to be psychologists, nobody tells us that we'll also have to be: secretary, marketing professional, content creator, accountant. We think we'll sit and talk to patients and that's it."*

> *"Can someone please teach me this as if I was 5 years old? I have a hard time understanding these things."*

**Product implication:** The product voice cannot sound like an accounting tool. It must speak in plain language, treat fiscal complexity as something the system absorbs rather than the user learns, and never make the user feel that not knowing was their fault.

---

## Behavioral Patterns Observed

| Pattern | Description |
|---|---|
| Silent assumption | Professionals assumed they were compliant because nobody had told them otherwise. The absence of a penalty felt like confirmation of compliance. |
| Shame-driven isolation | The fear of appearing incompetent prevented people from asking, even from each other. Everyone was embarrassed about the same thing, in isolation. |
| Avoidance through busyness | Fiscal obligations were not ignored through carelessness. They were deferred because the professional had no clear path to understanding them, and deferral felt safer than acting incorrectly. |
| Triggered activation | No one discovered the problem through their own monitoring. Discovery required an external event: a family conversation, a colleague's story, a tax notice. |
| Debt normalization | Among those who had already gone through regularization, paying off the debt over time was treated as an unfortunate but normal part of starting a private practice. |
| Invisible notifications | Tax authorities notify through government portals (e.g., e-CAC) that professionals have no reason to access if nobody told them those systems exist. Problems accumulate undetected not because professionals ignore warnings, but because they never receive them. |

---

## Implications for Product

The research shaped three foundational product decisions:

**Push over pull.** Professionals do not proactively check their fiscal status. A product that waits to be opened will not be used at the moment it's needed. Compasso must initiate contact. and do so through a channel professionals already check constantly (WhatsApp).

**Proof over self-reporting.** Professionals want to know that something was done correctly, not just that they clicked a checkbox. The anxiety behind the problem is about uncertainty, not effort. A validated receipt with government authentication codes addresses the actual fear.

**Clarity over completeness.** What professionals need is not access to all fiscal information, it's to know exactly what applies to them and exactly what to do about it. Compasso's onboarding must surface the specific obligations that apply to each user's profile, not a general overview of all possible obligations.

---

## A Note on This Research

This document reflects what was learned informally, through personal experience and peer conversations. It was not conducted as a structured study.

That informality is part of why it is credible. The conversations happened because the problem was real and the participants were not being asked to describe it academically. The silence that broke when the topic came up, the messages that followed, the stories that came out, those were not responses to interview questions. They were the problem making itself visible.

The product exists because of what was learned here.
